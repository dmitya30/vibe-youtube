## Narration

<!-- NARRATION_START -->

Your eyes hurt. The videos stopped being fun twenty minutes ago. But your thumb is still moving. Tonight, I’ll show you why stopping can feel harder when you’re already tired—and how to give the scroll an ending.

Researchers call one version bedtime procrastination: going to bed later than intended when nothing outside you keeps you up. But the delay can also happen after you’re already in bed. Those are not quite the same problem.

That distinction matters because the phone is not always keeping you from bed. Sometimes you are on the sofa, postponing the moment when the day officially ends. Other times, you made it under the blanket, turned off the light, and brought an endless feed with you.

The first scientific studies defined bedtime procrastination narrowly: you intended to go to bed, you could have gone to bed, and you delayed anyway. In one early study of 177 adults, people who reported more bedtime procrastination also reported less self-regulation and more insufficient sleep. But that study was correlational. It could identify a pattern, not prove one simple cause.

A later exploratory study of 400 teenagers separated delaying bedtime from delaying sleep while already in bed. The two were only weakly related in that sample. That does not settle the question for everyone, but it gives us a useful place to start: before trying to fix the behavior, notice which delay is actually happening.

Now look at the feed itself. A chapter ends. A movie reaches its credits. A friend says goodnight. A feed does none of those things. Every item arrives with the next item already waiting underneath it. The design does not force your thumb to move, but it gives your evening no natural final page.

And by this point, scrolling may not even feel good. It may simply be easier than deciding what happens next. Going to sleep means closing the day. It means accepting that the free part is over, tomorrow is closer, and anything unfinished will remain unfinished.

This is where the popular phrase revenge bedtime procrastination can sound persuasive. Some people do describe late-night delay as compensation: the day belonged to work, school, family, errands, or other people, so the night becomes the last piece that feels personally owned. Emotional regulation and compensation have also appeared as possible functions in a small behavioral study.

But possible is the important word. Not everyone is reclaiming autonomy. You may be avoiding tomorrow. You may be absorbed in a conversation. You may have an irregular schedule. You may be dealing with insomnia or another health issue. A useful explanation should help you observe the behavior, not diagnose you from a thumbnail.

Research on electronic media and sleep also needs careful wording. A systematic review covering 55 papers and more than 41,000 participants found that heavier electronic-media use was associated with poorer sleep outcomes. But much of this evidence is correlational and varied substantially between studies. The honest conclusion is not that one screen automatically destroys sleep. It is that timing, content, displacement, arousal, and habitual use can all matter—and reducing the entire problem to blue light misses most of the behavior.

So instead of promising a perfect nighttime routine, try one bounded experiment for three evenings.

First, name the delay.

Before changing anything, ask: am I avoiding going to bed, or am I already in bed and avoiding sleep? Write down the answer in one sentence. If the answer changes from night to night, that is useful information. You are looking for the location of the decision, not judging yourself for making it.

Second, create an ending before you need one.

Choose a short piece of genuinely wanted free time earlier in the evening. Give it content with a visible boundary: one saved video, ten pages, one episode, one game round, or a twenty-minute call. The activity does not need to be productive. Its job is to feel chosen and to end somewhere.

An endless feed can still be part of that block, but decide its endpoint before opening it. A timer alone may become another notification to dismiss. Pair the time with a physical next action: when the block ends, the phone goes to one specific landing place.

Third, write one if-then sentence.

If my chosen block ends, then I place the phone at its landing place before choosing anything else.

That sentence does not remove temptation. It moves the decision earlier, before you are lying in the dark negotiating with the next video. Small behavioral trials suggest that structured plans can reduce bedtime procrastination for some people, but this is still an experiment, not treatment and not a guarantee.

For three mornings, do not ask whether you executed the routine perfectly. Ask three narrower questions.

Did I know which kind of delay was happening?

Did my free time have a visible ending?

Did the phone reach its landing place before I started negotiating?

If the answer is no, change the design, not the insult you use against yourself. Maybe the free-time block was too late. Maybe the chosen activity never felt like yours. Maybe the landing place was within arm’s reach. Or maybe the problem is not a habit experiment at all and deserves professional attention.

The point is not to make every evening efficient. The point is to stop asking your most tired self to invent an ending inside something designed to continue.

Tonight, before the next scroll begins, decide where it ends.
