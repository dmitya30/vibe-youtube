## Voice-over

### 1. Cold open

You open your laptop to finish one thing.

Then a message appears. You close it.

A tab reminds you of something else.

You check your phone without even knowing why.

Thirty seconds later, the document is still there—but the thought you had is gone.

Maybe your attention span isn’t broken.

Maybe your task just has too many exits.

And if that’s the problem, forcing yourself to focus harder may be the wrong fix.

### 2. What “47 seconds” actually means

You may have heard that the average attention span is now just 47 seconds.

That number is real—but it is often explained incorrectly.

Researchers observed people using computers in their normal working environments. In recent studies, people spent an average of about 47 seconds on one screen before switching to another.

Not one idea. Not one project. One screen.

Opening a reference for the document you are writing counts as a switch. So does checking a message, looking at the clock, or opening a new tab because you suddenly need to know how many volcanoes Iceland has.

Forty-seven seconds is not a biological countdown after which your brain becomes incapable of attention. It is an observation of digital behavior in environments filled with possible switches.

And averages hide variation. Sometimes we stay with one screen for several minutes. Sometimes we leave almost immediately.

So the useful question is not:

“How do I force my brain to focus forever?”

It is:

“What keeps making this task so easy to leave?”

### 3. The two kinds of exits

Some exits are external.

A notification appears. Someone sends a message. A browser badge turns orange. A coworker asks a question.

These interruptions are easy to blame because we can see them arrive.

But many exits are self-created.

You wonder whether someone replied, so you check.

The current paragraph becomes difficult, so you open another tab.

You remember a small unrelated task and decide to do it now, before you forget.

Nothing interrupted you.

You interrupted yourself.

This explains why turning off notifications can help without solving the entire problem.

In one randomized intervention, participants disabled smartphone notifications for a week. But this did not significantly reduce how often they checked their phones or their total screen time.

Remove the alert, and the checking habit can remain.

At the same time, we should not pretend that every notification destroys twenty minutes of productivity.

In one laboratory task, smartphone notification sounds slowed responses by only a few milliseconds on average. The measured effect was small.

One sound is not a cognitive meteor strike.

The larger problem is the system around it: dozens of possible destinations, familiar checking habits, and difficult moments that make another screen feel more attractive.

An exit does not have to drag you away.

It only has to make leaving easier than continuing.

### 4. What a switch costs

Imagine that you are assembling a small model in your head.

You know what the paragraph is trying to say, which sentence feels wrong, and what should come next.

Then you switch to a message.

The model may not disappear, but it becomes harder to access. When you return, you have to reconstruct your position:

What was I doing?

What had I already decided?

What was the next move?

Researchers call the delay before continuing an interrupted task a resumption lag.

In experiments, people resumed faster when they had a brief opportunity to prepare for the interruption or when useful cues from the original task remained available.

This is why a document can be open directly in front of you while your mental position inside it is missing.

You may also have heard that it takes around 25 minutes to recover from an interruption.

That wording is misleading.

The field research measured how long it took people to return to the original project after moving through other activities. It does not mean every notification leaves every person unable to focus for exactly 25 minutes.

Sometimes you return quickly.

Sometimes one switch creates a chain:

Message. Email. Calendar. Search. Another message. Then the original document.

Interrupted work can even look productive because people compensate by working faster. In one experiment, participants completed interrupted tasks faster without a measured drop in quality—but reported more stress, frustration, effort, and time pressure.

The cost is not always visible in the finished document.

Sometimes it appears in the person finishing it.

### 5. The One Door Protocol

Instead of treating attention like a muscle that must be screamed at, change the architecture of the task.

For one session, give the task one obvious entrance and as few exits as possible.

This is the One Door Protocol.

It is not a clinically validated treatment or a magical productivity formula. It is a practical experiment built from what interruption research suggests about switching, cues, and returning to a task.

#### Step one: name one observable outcome

“Study biology” is not an observable outcome.

“Review chapter four” is better, but still leaves room for wandering.

“Create ten flashcards from chapter four” gives the session a visible finish line.

For writing:

“Draft a 150-word opening paragraph.”

For administration:

“Complete and send the form.”

Describe something you can see when it is done.

Now you do not have to keep deciding what “working” means.

#### Step two: close visible exits

Keep only what the outcome requires.

Close unrelated tabs. Hide badges. Put the phone somewhere that requires a deliberate movement to reach.

You do not have to transform your room into a silent monastery.

The goal is simply to add friction between an impulse and an exit.

If the phone is already in your hand, checking it is almost effortless.

If it is across the room, the impulse gets a few seconds to become visible.

That pause gives you a choice.

#### Step three: park unrelated impulses

You will remember things.

Buy toothpaste.

Reply to Alex.

Check whether Iceland really has that many volcanoes.

Trying not to think about these things can become another distraction. Acting on each thought is worse.

Keep a small parking list beside the task.

Write two or three words—just enough to recover the thought later—and continue.

The list is not a second project.

Do not organize it, decorate it, or turn it into a color-coded productivity ecosystem.

It is temporary storage for exits you chose not to take.

#### Step four: leave a return cue

Some interruptions are unavoidable.

Before leaving the task, write the next physical action.

Not:

“Continue report.”

Write:

“Compare the final two numbers in table three.”

Or:

“Explain why the second example contradicts the first.”

A useful return cue stores your position outside your head.

Research on interrupted tasks suggests that even a short opportunity to encode the next goal can make resumption easier.

You cannot prevent every interruption.

But you can make returning less expensive.

#### Step five: stop at a natural breakpoint

Focus is not the same thing as refusing to stop.

If you reach the end of a section, finish a problem, or complete the defined outcome, that may be a good place for a break.

Before leaving, create the next return cue.

Now the task has a handle.

When you come back, you do not need to rediscover where to grab it.

### 6. The 25-minute experiment

Do not rebuild your entire life around this protocol yet.

Test it once.

Choose one task you have been avoiding and define one observable outcome.

Set a timer for 25 minutes—not because 25 is a magical neurological number, but because it is long enough to observe your switching habits and short enough to attempt today.

Close visible exits.

Keep a parking list.

Then make a mark every time you want to leave the task.

Do not judge the impulse. Just record it.

At the end, look at the list.

Which exits were external?

Which ones did you create yourself?

Which appeared when the work became confusing, uncomfortable, or boring?

The goal is not to prove that you possess extraordinary discipline.

It is to make the exits visible.

Because once you can see the architecture of your attention, you can start changing it.

### 7. Ending

Your attention is not a purity test.

And it is probably not repaired by deleting every app, sitting in an empty room, or attempting a literal dopamine detox.

You do not need to become a person who never gets distracted.

You need a task that is easier to return to—and an environment where leaving requires a conscious choice.

Try one task, one outcome, and one door.

Then notice which exit you still try to take.

Messages? Tabs? Your phone?

Or something else?

## Claim limits

1. 47 seconds means observed average time on one screen before switching, not a biological attention limit.
2. The approximately 25-minute figure describes return to the original project after a chain of activities, not universal recovery time.
3. Notification-sound effects in the cited laboratory task were small.
4. Disabling notifications alone did not significantly reduce objective checking frequency or screen time in the cited intervention.
5. One Door Protocol is an evidence-informed editorial synthesis, not a clinically validated treatment.
6. The 25-minute experiment is a practical boundary, not a neurological standard.
7. Do not add brain-damage, dopamine-reset, addiction diagnosis, treatment or guaranteed-result claims.
